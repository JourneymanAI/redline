# Agent 2 — What Goes Wrong (Clause Types)

Scope: recurring clause types that actually burn people who sign contracts, leases, freelance
agreements, and terms of service. Ranked by how often they surface in real complaints, lawsuits,
and practitioner advice. Product hypothesis behind this research is NOT validated.

Method note: 11 web searches, 9 page-fetch attempts (4 succeeded, 5 returned 403/404 — several
law-firm blogs and FTC pages block automated fetches). Where a direct page read failed, findings
rest on the search-engine result summaries plus corroborating sources, and that is flagged.

---

## Ranked clause types

### 1. IP assignment / "work product" ownership grabs
- **Why it burns people:** Under US copyright law the freelancer/creator owns the work by
  default. A broad assignment or "work-for-hire" clause flips that — and the aggressive versions
  claim ownership of *everything used to make it*: reusable code libraries, design systems, fonts,
  internal tools, pre-existing IP. Worse, assignment "upon delivery" or "upon acceptance" (not
  "upon full payment") means the client can take the work, refuse to pay, and still own it, leaving
  the freelancer no leverage.
- **Evidence:** Multiple practitioner sources call IP ownership "the most frequently litigated
  issue in freelancer disputes." PayOdin's red-flag list ranks "Intellectual Property Grabs" #2,
  with the example clause claiming "all work product, including ... methods, and tools used in the
  creation thereof." Fiverr Workspace / freelance-advice consensus: assignment must be tied to
  payment clearing, "not before, not at delivery, not upon 'acceptance.'"
  - https://payodin.com/blog/freelance-contract-red-flags/
  - https://www.daeryunlaw.com/us/insights/freelancer-agreement-in-nyc
  - https://workspace.fiverr.com/faq/what-are-intellectual-property-rights
- **Who it hits hardest:** Freelancers and independent contractors (designers, developers,
  writers, consultants); also employees signing invention-assignment agreements that sweep in side
  projects.
- **How often it appears in complaints:** Very high among freelance/contractor disputes.
  Assessment based on repeated "most litigated" framing across independent freelance-law sources
  and its top-3 placement on every freelance red-flag list reviewed.

### 2. Auto-renewal / evergreen clauses
- **Why it burns people:** The contract silently renews for another full term unless the customer
  cancels inside a short notice window (often 30–90 days before renewal) that they've forgotten
  about. Miss it and you owe another year — or, on a commercial lease, another multi-year term.
  Frequently paired with a price bump at renewal and a hard-to-find cancellation process.
- **Evidence:** Large class-action history: LifeLock settled for $2.5M over auto-renewal
  practices under California law; McAfee agreed to pay up to $80M over auto-renewing subscriptions
  at higher-than-promised prices; similar suits against Tinder, Dropbox, Hulu, Blue Apron. At
  least 22 states have enacted statutory restrictions on auto-renewal terms. Complaints span HVAC
  contracts, lawn services, exterminators, gyms, software. In residential leases, failure to send
  a 60-day notice can auto-convert to month-to-month with rent up ~25% plus monthly admin fees.
  - https://www.multistate.us/insider/2017/4/6/auto-renewal-clauses-in-consumer-contracts-under-scrutiny-by-state-legislatures
  - https://stateline.org/2017/02/27/didnt-see-that-renewal-notice-some-states-are-trying-to-help/ (search summary only; page fetch 403'd)
  - https://www.lawinsider.com/clause/automatic-renewal-of-lease
- **Who it hits hardest:** Consumers with subscriptions, small businesses with vendor/service
  contracts, tenants (residential and commercial).
- **How often it appears in complaints:** Very high. Assessment based on the volume of class
  actions, the fact that 22+ state legislatures acted on it, and its consistent appearance in
  both consumer-protection and lease-review sources.

### 3. Vague scope of work + unlimited / undefined revisions
- **Why it burns people:** "Vague scope" language ("and any related work," "as Client may
  reasonably request") turns the agreed deliverable into a floor, not a ceiling. With no defined
  revision policy, the client requests changes indefinitely and the freelancer has no contractual
  basis to say no or bill more. This is the mechanism behind most freelance non-payment and
  "the project never ended" stories.
- **Evidence:** Practitioner estimate that the scope-of-work clause is "cited in an estimated
  60–70% of freelance payment and revision disputes" — the single most disputed element. PayOdin
  lists "Vague Scope Language" as red flag #1. Advice consensus: cap revisions at 2–3 rounds,
  define "revision" vs. "new direction," set a per-round fee beyond the cap.
  - https://www.jobbers.io/the-most-common-freelance-contract-clauses-that-prevent-disputes-ranked-by-frequency/ (search summary only; page fetch 403'd)
  - https://payodin.com/blog/freelance-contract-red-flags/
  - https://prolinker.com/blog/what-belongsin-freelance-contract
- **Who it hits hardest:** Freelancers and agencies on fixed-fee work.
- **How often it appears in complaints:** Highest among freelance disputes specifically
  (per the 60–70% figure), though it is a services-contract issue more than a consumer/lease one.

### 4. Unilateral termination for convenience (with no kill fee / no pay for work done)
- **Why it burns people:** "Client may terminate at any time, for any reason, without notice"
  with no obligation to pay for in-progress or completed-but-unbilled work. The client can pull
  the plug after weeks of blocked calendar, pay only for formally accepted milestones, and leave
  the freelancer with a hole in their pipeline. The protective counter — a kill fee of 25–50% of
  the remaining balance — is simply absent from most client-drafted templates.
- **Evidence:** PayOdin ranks "Unilateral Termination Without Compensation" red flag #4:
  "Client can cancel after weeks of completed work, pay nothing for uncompleted portions, and
  disrupt freelancer's income pipeline." Freelance-contract guides treat an explicit kill fee as
  one of the seven essential clauses precisely because its absence is so common.
  - https://payodin.com/blog/freelance-contract-red-flags/
  - https://work-club.com/freelance-contract-requirements-business-2026/
- **Who it hits hardest:** Freelancers, independent contractors, small agencies; also small
  suppliers on "termination for convenience" clauses in enterprise vendor contracts.
- **How often it appears in complaints:** High in freelance/contractor advice content;
  consistently in the top 5 red flags.

### 5. Indemnification / "defense and hold harmless" (uncapped)
- **Why it burns people:** The indemnifying party must fund the other side's legal defense and
  pay damages for covered claims *before any fault is decided*. Broad language ("any and all
  claims, damages, losses, and expenses") can make you liable for things outside your control —
  including the other party's own negligence. Many commercial liability insurance policies
  *exclude* contractual indemnification, so there's no coverage behind the obligation.
- **Evidence:** MHS Law: such clauses "have bankrupted small operations overnight"; classic
  fact pattern is a subcontractor forced to indemnify a general contractor for the GC's own
  negligence. "When a claim hits, the indemnifying party can be forced to fund the other side's
  defense and damages before any fault is decided," and "many liability policies exclude
  contractual indemnification." Courts otherwise treat contract liability as uncapped by default.
  - https://mhslaw.com/the-hidden-danger-in-your-contracts-indemnification-clauses-that-can-sink-a-small-business/
  - https://www.boughtonlaw.com/2024/10/understanding-liability-and-indemnity-clauses-in-commercial-contracts-why-they-matter-for-your-business/
  - https://payodin.com/blog/freelance-contract-red-flags/ (ranks "Unlimited Indemnification" red flag #3)
- **Who it hits hardest:** Small businesses, subcontractors, freelancers signing client
  paper — the smaller party almost always carries the indemnity.
- **How often it appears in complaints:** High in small-business and contractor legal advice;
  described as a "hidden danger" because signers don't notice it until a claim lands.

### 6. Limitation of liability / liability caps (one-sided or absent)
- **Why it burns people:** Two failure modes. (a) The clause caps the *vendor's* liability at
  fees paid (or excludes all consequential damages) while leaving the customer fully exposed — so
  when the vendor's failure causes real business loss, there's no recourse. (b) No cap at all,
  which courts read as unlimited liability, so a single bad outcome can exceed the entire contract
  value many times over.
- **Evidence:** "Unless otherwise agreed in the contract, courts generally will interpret a
  party's liability to the other under a contract to be uncapped and unlimited." SaaS
  contract-review guides list one-sided limitation-of-liability among the top red flags to
  renegotiate. Freelance guidance flags contracts where "if anything goes wrong, the freelancer
  is the one who pays even if it's the client's fault."
  - https://legal.thomsonreuters.com/en/insights/articles/indemnification-clauses-in-commercial-contracts
  - https://www.varisource.com/blog/saas-contract-red-flags-clauses-to-avoid
  - https://docket.acc.com/node/4168
- **Who it hits hardest:** Customers of SaaS/service vendors; small businesses buying software
  that runs core operations; freelancers on client paper with no mutual liability language.
- **How often it appears in complaints:** High in B2B/vendor-contract review content; usually
  discussed alongside indemnification as the paired "risk allocation" fight.

### 7. Mandatory arbitration + class-action waiver
- **Why it burns people:** Forces every dispute into private, individual arbitration and bans
  joining a class. When harm per person is small (a $35 fee, a $100 unauthorized charge) but
  systemic across thousands, no lawyer will take the individual case and the wrongdoing never gets
  aggregated — the company effectively escapes accountability. Also hides patterns of abuse from
  public view.
- **Evidence:** Wells Fargo used arbitration clauses to push customers with up to 3.5M fake
  accounts out of court, and tried to compel arbitration even after the fraud was public. Other
  documented examples: Sterling Jewelers (~70,000 women, pay/harassment claims into arbitration),
  Red Robin (2,600+ workers forced into individual arbitration on wage claims), Uber (600,000
  drivers' data-breach claims), Corinthian Colleges students, Epic Systems v. Lewis (Supreme
  Court, 2018). Post-Epic analysis: "more than half compelled plaintiffs to arbitrate."
  - https://www.durbin.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/-durbin-leading-democratic-senators-call-on-wells-fargo-to-end-use-of-forced-arbitration-to-allow-customers-to-seek-justice-in-court
  - https://fairarbitrationnow.org/wells-fargos-use-forced-arbitration-deny-consumers-justice/
  - https://centerjd.org/content/fact-sheet-cases-tossed-out-court-because-forced-arbitration-causes-and-class-action-bans
- **Who it hits hardest:** Consumers (banking, telecom, rental cars, subscriptions), employees
  (wage theft, discrimination), students.
- **How often it appears in complaints:** Very high as a *systemic* consumer/worker-protection
  grievance; near-universal in consumer terms of service. Less often the thing an individual
  notices at signing, more often the wall they hit when they try to sue.

### 8. Non-compete / non-solicit
- **Why it burns people:** Prevents a worker from taking a better job in their field for months
  or years after leaving, often across an unreasonably broad geography and industry definition.
  Suppresses wages (no credible outside offer to negotiate against) and traps people in jobs they
  want to leave. Frequently imposed on people with no trade secrets to protect — hourly and
  low-wage workers.
- **Evidence:** Non-competes bind roughly 1 in 5 US workers (~30 million people). FTC
  enforcement example: Gateway Services (pet cremation) imposed non-competes on ~1,800 employees
  "ranging from executives to hourly workers," barring them from the pet-cremation industry
  anywhere in the US for a year. FTC's rulemaking record found non-competes "hurt workers and
  harm competition" by blocking moves to higher pay. (FTC's 2024 blanket ban was vacated in 2025;
  the agency shifted to targeted enforcement, and many state laws still limit or void them.)
  - https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftc-proposes-rule-ban-noncompete-clauses-which-hurt-workers-harm-competition (search summary only; page fetch 403'd)
  - https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/260429-ftc-noncompete-enforcement
  - https://www.velaw.com/insights/no-more-non-competes-part-five-ftc-abandons-rule-prohibiting-non-compete-agreements-but-advances-targeted-enforcement/
- **Who it hits hardest:** Employees across the wage spectrum; also freelancers hit with
  "don't work for our competitors for 24 months" clauses.
- **How often it appears in complaints:** Very high as a labor-market grievance (30M workers,
  federal rulemaking, active state legislation). PayOdin ranks "Overreaching Non-Competes" as a
  freelance red flag as well.

### 9. Fee escalators / price-increase clauses
- **Why it burns people:** The contract lets the vendor raise price at each renewal — sometimes
  automatically, without the customer's explicit approval — by a fixed percentage or "CPI or X%,
  whichever is greater." Compounded over a multi-year term the cost balloons with no change in
  what's delivered.
- **Evidence:** Reported SaaS price hikes of 9–25% annually against IT budgets growing ~2.8%;
  5–15% annual increases "normalized." Worked example: a $1M contract with a 15% annual renewal
  increase reaches $1.75M by year 4 vs. $1.12M with a 4% cap — a $630K difference over three
  years. "Auto-escalation clauses allow price increases at renewal without explicit approval."
  In residential leases, month-to-month conversion commonly carries a ~25% rent jump.
  - https://upperedge.com/contract-negotiations/hidden-renewal-clause-that-could-inflate-your-saas-costs/
  - https://www.pocsheet.com/blog/price-increase-clause-cpi-escalation
  - https://www.varisource.com/blog/saas-contract-red-flags-clauses-to-avoid
- **Who it hits hardest:** Businesses on multi-year SaaS/vendor contracts; tenants on leases
  with renewal escalators.
- **How often it appears in complaints:** High in procurement / vendor-management content;
  a top item on SaaS contract red-flag lists. Less visible to individual consumers.

### 10. Personal guarantees
- **Why it burns people:** An individual (usually the owner) personally promises to pay if the
  business defaults, so the LLC/corp liability shield is bypassed. If the business signs a 10-year
  commercial lease and closes after year 1, the landlord can sue the guarantor personally for the
  entire remaining balance and pursue personal bank accounts, home equity, and other assets —
  potentially forcing personal bankruptcy.
- **Evidence:** "If you signed a 10-year lease and personally guaranteed it, you could be on the
  hook for the full balance — even if your business closes after a year." Landlords can pursue the
  business and all guarantors "jointly and severally" and go after "personal bank accounts,
  property, and other assets." "Good guy guaranty" / limited guaranty exist as negotiated
  alternatives but are not the default.
  - https://www.findlaw.com/legalblogs/small-business/what-is-a-personal-guarantee-commercial-lease-and-how-to-get-out-of-one/
  - https://www.jdelaw.nyc/personal-guarantee-commercial-lease
  - https://staceyromberg.com/blog/what-business-owners-should-know-about-personal-guarantees-for-commercial-leases/
- **Who it hits hardest:** Small-business owners signing commercial leases, equipment leases,
  vendor credit lines, and SBA/business loans.
- **How often it appears in complaints:** High within the small-business commercial-lease
  context specifically; multiple law firms publish "how to get out of one" guides, which signals
  recurring pain. Narrower audience than the consumer clauses above.

### 11. Unilateral amendment ("we can change these terms at any time")
- **Why it burns people:** The company reserves the right to rewrite the agreement whenever it
  wants, effective on posting, with "continued use" deemed acceptance — often with no notice.
  The customer never meaningfully agreed to the terms they're now bound by, and there's little
  recourse short of stopping use of the service. Legal scholars argue it can make the whole
  contract "illusory."
- **Evidence:** These clauses are "ubiquitous" and appear in the terms of Facebook, Twitter/X,
  Instagram, and Google — "the industry standard." Courts have "repeatedly refused to enforce
  these silent changes when notice was buried, when the user had no real chance to consent, or
  when the clause itself was so one-sided it made the whole agreement illusory," and several
  federal/state appellate courts have struck down unilateral amendments.
  - https://medium.com/@jlkoepke/we-can-change-these-terms-at-anytime-the-detritus-of-terms-of-service-agreements-712409e2d0f1
  - https://ccbjournal.com/articles/unilaterally-amended-terms-of-service-agreements-put-customers-in-a-bind
  - https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=e79c3c06-ec1e-4040-934f-b0fb21d54eb0
- **Who it hits hardest:** Consumers using online platforms and apps; also SaaS business
  customers whose vendor changes the DPA / acceptable-use / SLA terms mid-contract.
- **How often it appears in complaints:** Near-universal *presence* in consumer terms of
  service; a standing academic and consumer-advocate criticism. Rarely the trigger of an
  individual dispute, often the reason a dispute can't be won.

### 12. Confidentiality overreach / overbroad NDA
- **Why it burns people:** Confidentiality drafted so broadly it's less about protecting real
  trade secrets and more about silencing the signer about everything: it blocks a freelancer from
  showing the work in a portfolio or naming the client as a reference (killing their ability to
  get the next job), and it can chill employees from discussing pay, working conditions, or
  reporting illegal conduct. Indefinite/perpetual duration and high liquidated-damages figures
  make people "terrified to come forward."
- **Evidence:** PayOdin ranks "Excessive Confidentiality Clauses" a red flag: "prevents
  portfolio display and industry references, eliminating professional credibility-building
  opportunities." On the employment side: "High liquidated damages can create a dynamic where
  employees are terrified to come forward even about illegal company behavior"; courts often
  reject perpetual NDAs (reasonable term usually 1–5 years); overbroad terms may "infringe upon
  employee rights, including the right to discuss workplace conditions or seek legal counsel."
  - https://payodin.com/blog/freelance-contract-red-flags/
  - https://aaronhall.com/legal-risks-of-over-broad-non-disclosure-agreements/
  - https://epic.law/perpetual-or-even-long-term-confidentiality-obligation-periods-can-kill-employment-ndas/
  - https://www.workplacefairness.org/non-disclosure-agreements/
- **Who it hits hardest:** Freelancers (portfolio/reference loss), employees and contractors
  (whistleblower chill, pay-secrecy).
- **How often it appears in complaints:** Moderate-to-high. Consistently on freelance red-flag
  lists; a recurring employment-law topic, amplified since ~2018 by #MeToo-era scrutiny of NDAs.

## Clause types the evidence surfaced that weren't on the starting list

- **Vague / open-ended scope of work.** Not a "clause type" in the classic risk-allocation
  sense, but by practitioner estimate the single largest driver of freelance contract disputes
  (60–70% of payment/revision disputes). Ranked #3 above because the evidence was too strong to
  omit. Source: https://www.jobbers.io/the-most-common-freelance-contract-clauses-that-prevent-disputes-ranked-by-frequency/ (search summary).
- **Unlimited / undefined revisions.** Closely tied to scope; treated as its own red flag by
  freelance sources ("a blank check drawn on your time"). Folded into #3.
- **Absence of a kill fee.** The protective counterpart to termination-for-convenience; its
  omission is itself the recurring harm. Folded into #4.
  Source: https://work-club.com/freelance-contract-requirements-business-2026/
- **Subjective acceptance / "payment contingent on Client's complete satisfaction."** Creates
  indefinite payment withholding because satisfaction is legally unprovable. PayOdin red flag #5.
  Source: https://payodin.com/blog/freelance-contract-red-flags/
- **Non-disparagement clauses.** One-sided ("Contractor agrees not to make any statements ...
  that disparage Client"), no reciprocal protection, and specifically prevents a freelancer from
  warning peers about an abusive client.
  Source: https://www.dott.legal/blog/freelance-contract-red-flags (via search summary) and
  https://www.smvrtlegal.com/smvrt-legal-blog/freelance-contract-red-flags
- **Perpetual / indefinite duration** as a standalone defect — it shows up on confidentiality,
  non-compete, and indemnity clauses alike and is a common reason courts refuse to enforce them.
  Source: https://epic.law/perpetual-or-even-long-term-confidentiality-obligation-periods-can-kill-employment-ndas/
- **AI-usage terms** are now cited as an "essential" modern freelance clause whose absence
  causes disputes; too new to rank, noted for completeness.
  Source: https://work-club.com/freelance-contract-requirements-business-2026/

Starting-list items I found comparatively little dedicated complaint evidence for: **exclusivity
clauses** and **late fees / penalties** as litigated problems in their own right (see below).

## What I could not find

- **A single quantitative cross-domain ranking.** No source ranks all clause types by complaint
  frequency across contracts + leases + freelance + ToS together. The only hard percentages found
  are freelance-specific (scope of work ~60–70% of freelance disputes; IP "most litigated").
  The overall ranking above is my synthesis from qualitative weight of evidence, not a measured
  frequency table.
- **Primary Reddit / forum threads.** The Reddit-targeted search returned blog aggregators, not
  actual r/freelance or r/smallbusiness threads, and I did not spend budget fetching raw Reddit.
  No first-person horror-story quotes were captured.
- **Primary consumer-agency documents.** FTC press releases and the non-compete NPRM fact sheet
  returned 403 to automated fetch; findings for non-competes rest on the search summary plus
  law-firm secondary coverage (Morrison Foerster, Vinson & Elkins). No CFPB source was retrieved
  directly.
- **Late fees / penalty clauses** as a standalone recurring harm: only tangential evidence
  (mentioned as a needed *protective* clause for freelancers, and lease early-termination
  penalties "exceeding actual damages"). Did not find a body of complaints or a liquidated-damages
  unenforceability line of cases within budget.
- **Exclusivity clauses:** almost no dedicated complaint evidence surfaced. Listed on the
  starting hypothesis but under-supported by what I found.
- **Stateline auto-renewal article and Jobbers freelance-ranking article** could not be read
  directly (403); their content is represented via search-engine summaries only and should be
  re-verified before use.
- **Dollar-weighted "how much does each clause cost victims"** data: not found except the SaaS
  escalator worked example ($630K over 3 years on a $1M contract) and the class-action settlement
  figures (LifeLock $2.5M, McAfee up to $80M).
